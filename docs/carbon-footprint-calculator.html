Food-Waste Processing's Carbon Footprint

Food waste can be treated in several ways. The carbon footprint of each method depends not only on the treatment process itself, but also on collection, transport, water use, energy consumption, biological stability, by-products, and final disposal.

How Treatment Processes Differ

Process Mechanism Carbon-footprint drivers Main characteristics Implications
Incineration and Landfill (“Burn and Bury”) Food waste is mixed with general waste, transported, incinerated, and the ash is sent to landfill. Collection, truck transport, incineration, ash transport, and landfill disposal. Common and centralised, but wet food waste adds moisture burden during incineration. Typically carbon-intensive because emissions arise across transport, burning, and final disposal.
On-Site Maceration (“Grind and Flush”) Food waste is ground with water into slurry and discharged into the wastewater system. Potable water use, high-COD wastewater treatment, aeration energy, sludge handling, sludge drying, sludge incineration, and possible methane or nitrous oxide release. Simple at the point of use, but the waste burden is shifted downstream. May appear clean on-site, but can increase the carbon burden in the wastewater treatment system.
Centralised Anaerobic Co-Digestion with Sludge (“Co-Digestion”) Source-separated food waste is transported to a central facility, processed into bio-pulp, and digested with used-water sludge to produce biogas. Specialised transport, pre-processing, AD operation, process control, biogas recovery, and digestate or sludge handling. Biologically sensitive; methane yield can vary with feedstock, pH, temperature, inhibitors, and microbial balance. Can deliver carbon benefits when biogas yield is stable and efficiently used, but actual performance can vary.
On-Site Aerobic Digestion, such as Bio Nexa DryLoop 100 (“Dry Composting”) Food waste is treated at source using oxygen, heat, mixing, and microbial activity to produce a dry, stabilised compost-like output. Electricity for dehydration, mixing, airflow, and process control. Generally stable, with fast-growing aerobic microbes and a more resilient biological process. Avoids wet-waste transport, sewer discharge, high-COD wastewater treatment, and downstream sludge handling; footprint is largely linked to electricity use and grid emission factor.

Effect of grid decarbonisation:

Anaerobic digestion retains a carbon advantage over aerobic dry digestion at low grid-emission factors only if biogas energy is fully credited and methane leakage, digestate, and residue burdens are minimal. For Singapore, with a current grid-emission factor of about 0.402 kg CO₂e/kWh (EMA 2024), on-site aerobic dry digestion and centralised anaerobic digestion with biogas co-generation approach parity when the grid factor declines to roughly 0.25–0.30 kg CO₂e/kWh.

Interested in more details

1. Incineration and Landfill — “Burn and Bury”

This is the most common food waste pathway in many cities.

In this method, food scraps are not separated at source. They are thrown away together with general municipal waste, collected by waste trucks, and transported to a central facility. The waste is then compressed and incinerated. After incineration, the remaining solid residue, mainly ash, is transported again and buried at a landfill site.

From a carbon-footprint perspective, emissions arise from several stages: waste collection, truck transport, incineration, ash transport, and final landfill disposal. Because food waste contains a high amount of moisture, additional energy is used to heat and evaporate water during incineration.

2. On-Site Maceration — “Grind and Flush”

In this method, food scraps are separated at source and fed into a grinder. The grinder breaks the food waste into small particles and mixes it with water, often potable water, to form a slurry. The slurry is then discharged into the wastewater system.

This slurry is not ordinary wastewater. It can have chemical oxygen demand, or COD, about 70 to 80 times higher than normal municipal wastewater. COD measures how much oxygen is needed to break down organic matter in water. The higher the COD, the greater the treatment burden.

From a carbon-footprint perspective, maceration does not remove the waste burden. It shifts much of it from solid-waste management to wastewater treatment. The water reclamation plant requires additional energy for aeration and may produce additional sludge. That sludge then needs to be handled, dried, incinerated, and disposed of as ash. During wastewater treatment, greenhouse gases such as methane and nitrous oxide may also be released.

3. Centralised Anaerobic Co-Digestion with Sludge — “Co-Digestion”

Co-digestion requires food waste to be separated at source and transported by specialised trucks to a central facility. There, the food waste is processed into bio-pulp and mixed with used-water sludge. The mixture then undergoes anaerobic digestion, or AD, where organic matter is broken down without oxygen to produce biogas.

The main carbon benefit is that biogas can be used as energy. This can offset fossil-fuel energy use and is often counted as a carbon credit or negative-emission benefit in the carbon calculation.

However, anaerobic digestion is biologically sensitive. It relies on a tightly balanced microbial chain, ending with slow-growing methanogens that produce methane. If food waste composition, temperature, pH, or inhibitors such as ammonia, sulphides, salts, detergents, antibiotics, or metals disturb this balance, acids can accumulate, pH can fall, and methane yield can drop.

From a carbon-footprint perspective, co-digestion can perform well when biogas yield is stable and efficiently used. Its actual carbon performance, however, depends heavily on transport distance, feedstock consistency, process control, and real biogas recovery.

4. On-Site Aerobic Digestion, such as Bio Nexa DryLoop 100 — “Dry Composting”

In this method, food waste is treated directly at source, instead of being transported away as wet waste or flushed into the sewer. The system uses oxygen, heat, mixing, and microbial activity to break down food waste into a dry, stabilised compost-like output.

Aerobic digestion is generally a stable process because it relies on a broader and more resilient microbial ecosystem. Aerobic bacteria grow and recover quickly. The biology is also simpler, as organic matter is directly oxidised with oxygen.

From a carbon-footprint perspective, on-site aerobic digestion avoids several high-emission steps: wet-waste transport, slurry discharge, high-COD wastewater treatment, and downstream sludge handling. Its main energy use is dehydration. If electricity is the primary energy source, the grid emission factor will largely determine the process footprint, because a well-controlled aerobic process should not generate significant methane or nitrous oxide.
